16. Feature proposals and the approval gate (Daniel, Nov 7; approved Nov 8; amended Nov 13)
Daniel asked on Nov 7 (project chat 17:47 and 17:58 PT, and 18:07 PT in the thread "Write the feature proposal gate into Eng directions") that a new feature reach him as a proposal he can take in: the plan and the experience a user will have, shown with mocks, user journeys and diagrams (system design, data model, breaking changes), the trade-offs and implications spelled out, and every important technical aspect stated in plain words with nothing dropped and pointers into the detailed spec where he wants more. The detailed technical spec stays for the implementation agents and sits next to the proposal. Codex Astra reviews every proposal before Daniel sees it. Where Daniel has decisions to make, the proposal carries a surface where he makes them. His approval of the proposal gates implementation: no thread builds a new feature until he has approved its proposal. The project instructions carry a one-paragraph summary; this section is the rule, and the coordinator repeats it in the starting instructions of every feature thread. Daniel approved this section, with decisions D1 to D8 of the proposal "Feature proposal packets" as recommended, on Nov 8 at 23:31 UTC in that thread (packet v6, build e42a2da, spec draft v7 f4d0fab6). The packet and its record are in the Proposals folder below.
What needs a proposal. A new or expanded capability: anything a user, a developer or an operator will see or use that no approved proposal covers, including a screen, a flow, a tool, an endpoint, a record type or lexicon, an integration, a background job or a new service. Classify by what the work changes, not by its label: a "fix", a "security update", a "refactor" or a "dependency bump" that adds a capability, a provider, a permission or a data store is a feature. Maintenance that adds no capability and stays within approved behavior and recorded decisions needs no proposal and follows the rest of this Doc: fixes that restore specified behavior, copy changes inside an approved design, dependency and security updates, refactors that change no behavior, dead-code cleanup (section 15), tests and docs. A thread relying on an earlier approval cites it. A request from Daniel for a specific change approves the outcome, not the solution: a small change inside an approved design is done at once, with one line in the thread saying what will change; a new or expanded capability starts a proposal, in the short form when it is small. A new service or infrastructure capability needs a proposal too: a DNS, Railway or Access sentence (section 3) permits that operation and never approves the design behind it, and proposal approval never replaces the sentence. A thread that cannot tell which side its work falls on treats it as a feature or asks Daniel in one line in its own thread, and starts no implementation until it knows.
Transition. Approvals Daniel gave before Nov 7 stand for their recorded scope; the thread records the message, the covered work and the open items before relying on one. Planning or starting work is not approval, and existing holds stay (section 10: nothing in the portal's Event sync is built until Daniel calls the open decisions). Work in flight with no recorded approval stops implementing on the day this section takes effect and gets a proposal, in the short form where the feature is small; implementation resumes only on approval. The PR check under "After approval" applies to every feature PR still unmerged on that day, not only to PRs opened later.
Before approval. A thread may research, read code, draft the proposal and the spec, make mocks and diagrams, and run isolated experiments that answer a named question of the proposal, each with its question and its stopping point written down, touching nothing shared or live (a sandbox, a scratch checkout never pushed, a disposable account). It may not implement the feature for delivery anywhere, in a branch, a PR, an uncommitted tree or the project files included; may not merge, deploy or change DNS, Railway, Access, the PDS or any live account or service for it; may not publish a lexicon or record; may not hand the build to an implementation agent such as Codex; and may not send anything outside the project. Existing app renders, static mocks and isolated prototypes serve the proposal; a design or copy approval (design-directions skill) does not authorize the build.
The medium. The proposal is one self-contained HTML page, the proposal packet, published as a private claude.ai artifact so Daniel reads it on the web and on his phone, with Mermaid diagrams rendered to inline SVG (sources kept beside them), mocks embedded, detail folded under headings he can open, and links into the detailed spec by heading. The detailed spec stays a pageless Google Doc written under the "Technical spec writing playbook". Every version of the packet is also filed as a record bundle in Drive (next bullet) and rendered to PNG at 1440 and 390 wide for Codex and for the record. The packet's HTML source under /mnt/project-files/<area>/<date>/proposal/ is the canonical copy; the artifact and the Drive files are made from it. The feature-proposal skill holds the template, the build script (Mermaid from npm, Playwright renders, PDF) and the pre-send checks; threads do not hand-build packets once the skill exists. This section takes effect for feature threads on the day the coordinator announces in the project chat that the skill is saved, its template, build script and pre-send checks are in place, and the answer route approved under D2 has worked end to end at least once (record, "recorded <id>" message, read-back, confirmation card); until then a thread that must propose copies the prototype packet "Feature proposal packets" and its build script from the project files, which are the template's first version.
Where things live. Packets and their specs belong to the folder Proposals under Iriai/Iriai Eng in Drive (https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1QivrikScMKf-9yI-OBEONdS3IUFc0Rqm), one subfolder per feature named "<Feature> — proposal · <workbook IDs>" (IDs as in section 2), holding the record bundle per version (the HTML uploaded as a file, not converted; the PDF and the 1440 and 390 PNGs, which Drive can preview; the diagram sources; the approval record once there is one; until an upload lands, the approved build stays frozen by its hash in the project files, the source fragment, the built HTML and BUILD.txt, and the thread notes the pending upload in the thread, because the hash in the approval record is what pins the version, not where the copy sits) and the spec Doc "<Feature> — technical specification · <workbook IDs>". Specs written before Nov 7 stay where they are. Mocks, diagram sources, the review pack and its triage go under /mnt/project-files/<area>/<date>/ with a README listing the screens, as in portal/2026-10-31. Every Drive change is logged under agent rules section 3, one row per file per run; the feature's workbook row carries the packet link and its status, logged per the workbook-log skill.
What the packet contains. Each part is present in every packet; a part with nothing to say reads "none" with a one-line reason so Daniel can see it was considered. The first phone screen (390 by 844) shows the packet's name, status and build, what Daniel is asked to approve, the problem in one sentence, and the numbered decision table with the recommendations; the header details fold. The build checks this with a first-screen render. The parts, in order:
0 Header: feature name, workbook IDs, product(s), thread, packet status and version, spec link and the spec revision the packet describes, mocks folder, Drive record, decide-by date and what the thread does if unanswered, and Daniel's ask quoted with its date and channel.
1 The ask and the decisions: what Daniel is asked to approve in two or three sentences; the numbered decision table (one line per question, the recommendation, the status label the spec playbook uses: Agreed with the message it came from, Proposed, Open tagged before approval or before launch, Deferred with its reopen trigger); one-way doors flagged; the biggest risk or breaking change in one line; what approval starts and what it does not (merging, deploying, DNS, Railway and Access sentences, publishing a lexicon); how to answer. This is the decision surface: each decision has its controls here. Added Nov 13 (decision impact; approved packet "Decision impact in packets" v6, build fa7636a): Every option of every decision, the recommended one included, states what picking it means in five rows: what Daniel does, what gets built, the main risk, what changing it later costs, and where the packet reads differently. The page reads as Daniel's selection and shows the recommendation beside every place where the two differ. Under any selection the page states nothing that the selection makes false. A packet declares how its decisions relate, and the page holds his picks to those relations. These hold in the short form too, for the decisions it has.
2 The problem and the goal: who hits the problem today and why the status quo fails, who it is for, the goals, and how we will know it worked.
3 The experience: the user journey as numbered steps, the happy path first, then no access, error, retry, empty and loading states; a mock per step at 390 wide, 1440 in the appendix, under a contact sheet of every screen at both widths; before and after at equal width when an existing screen changes, changed regions outlined; lettered options where a decision is visible; every image labeled with its provenance (app build, HTML mock, sketch, diagram); Corgo screens come from the app build where the screen already exists; a new scene or screen with no app render yet is shown as a static mock or as a render from a bounded, isolated prototype allowed under "Before approval", labeled as such, and the app render follows after approval. A feature without a screen shows the actor's journey (developer, other app, operator) as numbered steps with a worked example: the sample record, request and response before and after. "None" is never allowed here.
4 How it works: the parts and what each does, in plain words, with evidence tags ([code] built and run; [record] seen in this project's own files, runs or threads; [docs] stated in the platform's documentation, with the file cited; [planned]; [unverified]) and a spec pointer per paragraph. Diagrams, each with a title, a key, labeled arrows, at most about seven boxes, a one-sentence caption saying what it shows, its decision numbers and a spec pointer: 4a a context diagram of the people, Iriai products and outside systems around the feature, no technology names, colored unchanged, new and changed, with a table of what crosses each interface (always); 4b a sequence diagram of the main flow with its main failure branch, and the same steps as a numbered list for the phone (when more than one component talks); 4c the data model as an ERD of the touched entities with key attributes, a plain-English list of the relationships, who can see each entity and how long it is kept (when stored data, records or lexicons change; otherwise "No data changes, because ..."); 4d the lifecycle as a state diagram with the user action on each arrow and the first irreversible write marked (when there is a lifecycle); 4e trust boundaries, the same boxes colored by zone (user's device, Iriai, the user's PDS, third parties) with the data category on each crossing arrow (when personal data, credentials, payments or a third party are involved); 4f where it runs, the deployment change (when hosting changes). Then limits, dependencies and what happens on failure.
5 Scope fence: what is in the first build; no-gos, the things a reasonable person would expect that this will not do, with the reason; deferred follow-ups with the trigger that reopens each; what is cut first if effort runs over.
6 Options and trade-offs: a table whose first row is always "do nothing or do it later" with its consequences, then each real alternative, with what the user gets, breaking changes, effort and cost, reversibility and risk, the recommended row marked; the drawbacks of the recommendation; rejected ideas kept with one reason each so later threads do not re-propose them.
7 Changes and breakage: a compatibility ledger of every contract touched (lexicon, HTTP route, schema, client-server protocol, URL, event payload, webhook) classified additive, deprecating or breaking, with who is affected, the overlap window, the sunset date, the migration and the undo; the ATProto lexicon evolution rules checked on every lexicon change; an old-client against new-server table when a client protocol changes; the migration timeline (expand, dual-write and backfill, verify, contract) with the point of no return and the evidence needed before it; the CHANGELOG entries the changes require. When nothing breaks, it reads "No breaking changes: checked <contracts>, all additive".
8 Implications: one row per concern, each a one-line answer with an evidence tag and a spec pointer: data stored and who can see it (including what lands in a public ATProto repo); security (threats considered, mitigations, assumptions, accepted risks Daniel is signing); rollout and rollback (stages, flags with removal dates, the rollback action and how long it takes); how we will know it is working and what alerts us; running cost today, after launch and at about ten times the use, as ranges; new vendors (purpose, data sent, fail open or closed, exit path); platform rules and terms (App Store, ATProto and Bluesky alpha terms, third-party permissions); accessibility and performance; effects on other features and products; earlier decisions of Daniel's this departs from, each named with where it is recorded, and each reversal made an explicit numbered decision (agent rules section 9); what reaches production users and when; what is hard to undo, with the cheapest way to postpone each one-way door; the sentences Daniel will have to type afterwards (DNS, Railway, Access, exact-copy confirmations), grouped so he types once. Rows are never deleted.
9 Risks: three to five lines of "it is N weeks later and this failed because ...", each with the mitigation or the accepted risk; estimate risks and the spike that firms the number.
10 Plan and gates: proposal approved, spec final, PRs (each through PR review with a risk rating; low merges, medium or high needs Daniel's merge word), staging preview with a page feedback link, release; effort as a range with what firms it; who builds what; dependencies on other threads with their status; hard outside dates; how Daniel will see it working (a staging page, a test run, a demo, an app build).
11 Review before the gate: who reviewed (Codex Astra with model and CLI version, or the independent Claude reviewer when the Mac is offline, named), the verdict, findings by severity, their dispositions, "decision list checked: N added", claims the reviewer could not verify, whether the review ran again after blockers, a one-line summary of the pre-send checks, and a link to the verbatim review and the triage.
12 Decision record and approval: per decision the context in two lines, the options considered and why they lost, the consequences, who decided and the link to Daniel's words with the timestamp, and "supersedes" links for reversals; Open items with owner and tier; Deferred items with their reopen trigger; the approval record (status, date, his words quoted); amendments after approval appended here, dated, each marked Pending or Approved (with the link to Daniel's words once approved) and whether it needed re-approval; the current view shows the approved scope and the pending amendments apart, so an implementer never reconstructs history to know what is operative.
13 Coverage and pointers into the spec: every consequential requirement and exception of the spec mapped to where the packet says it in plain words, or marked implementation-only with one line on why omitting it cannot change Daniel's understanding or decision; each pointer shows the spec heading's number and title and links to the heading (never a page number: the Docs are pageless); the reverse check that every packet claim exists in the spec; the spec revision and the date the links were checked. The reviewer checks the exclusions as well as the mappings.
14 Glossary, only when terms remain unexplained where they first appear.
15 Appendix, folded on the phone and expanded in the PNG renders for Codex: diagram sources, 1440 renders, wide tables, reproducibility notes (branch, commit, render settings), the provenance list of images, links. Nothing that affects a decision lives only here.
Short form: for a small, reversible, user-visible change with no data or contract change, parts 0 to 3 on one screen (ask, decisions, problem, before and after), then parts 5, 6 and 8 in concise form (a scope line with the no-gos, an options table even when it has two rows, the implications table), the review card and the spec pointer; the remaining parts may be combined or read "not applicable: <reason>" per part, never dropped silently. Smallness never removes a trade-off, a performance or accessibility effect, or a technical fact that could affect the decision.
Writing for Daniel. Short sentences; each technical term explained where it first appears; numbers in place of adjectives; a picture wherever a picture is clearer than a paragraph; the recommendation before the detail. Every technical fact that could affect his decision is in the packet, in plain words: correctness, compatibility, dependencies, failure and recovery, limits, cost, data and permissions, and anything else that matters for this feature; this list is not exhaustive. A precise value or distinction appears when it matters to the decision (a credential that stays valid for 600 seconds after removal is a packet fact, not an implementation detail); exact syntax stays in the spec only when leaving it out loses no meaning. One set of plain numbers for the decisions, used everywhere (the table, the badges on mocks and diagrams, the record), so "approve except 3" is never ambiguous. The default reading, the text visible with every fold closed, is about 1,500 words before the appendix; the build counts it and the count is stated in the review card; a packet that must run longer folds detail under headings rather than cutting it. Diagrams are checked at 390 wide before every publish: a diagram whose labels cannot be read at 390 carries an "in words" list beside it that says everything the picture says, and a link to its full-size image; the 390 record render shows every diagram whole.
Review before the gate. The packet and the spec go through the adversarial review in section 1 before the ask, following the codex-review skill for the pack, the run, the triage and the report: Codex Astra on Daniel's Mac over Remote Control, or the independent Claude reviewer when the Mac is offline, named in the packet's review card and in the ask. The pack holds the packet HTML and its 1440 and 390 renders, the diagram sources, the spec exported as Markdown with its revision, the mocks, and the earlier decisions of Daniel's that bear on the feature. The brief tells the reviewer to find: technical points in the spec that the packet leaves out, softens or states wrongly (part 13 is the checklist, exclusions included); journeys and mocks that disagree with each other or with the spec, and missing failure and empty states; trade-offs that treat a real alternative unfairly; implications left out; decisions missing from the decision list, and recommendations that silently override a standing direction of Daniel's; conflicts with earlier decisions; and passages Daniel could not follow without opening the spec. Every blocker and should-fix is resolved under section 6 of the codex-review skill before the ask; a disputed blocker or protected finding goes to Daniel; a deferral is not a disposition for a blocker or should-fix. From round 3 the step back applies, and a fix round may not make the packet or spec longer unless the triage explains why (codex-review section 6a). The review runs again on the new build after a blocker is fixed, and on every later version that changes scope, experience, cost, data, security, compatibility, dependencies or anything hard to undo. The pre-send checks (mocks consistent across screens, contact sheets regenerated, 390 renders readable, every diagram titled and captioned, every spec link resolving) run before the pack is built.
Asking for approval and the decision surface. The thread posts one ask in its own thread, opening with the ask itself: the packet link, the version and build hash, the spec revision, the numbered decisions with the recommendation for each, the review result in one line each (who, how many findings by severity, what was fixed, what was rejected and why), what approval lets the thread start, the three ways to answer, and the exact reply that approves the listed scope. The packet's part 1 carries the decision surface: for each decision, controls Daniel uses to pick an option or the recommendation and to add a note, and one control for the packet as a whole (approve as recommended, approve with my choices above, approve in part, request changes, not yet). Threads never write to the record store. The store cannot by itself tell Daniel's act from any other writer at the owner level (on a project artifact that level is every member who can post in the project) or from the thread-written page code acting in his session, so a record is a draft of his answer, never the answer itself. Each Record creates a new receipt document under a fresh random id of 96 bits that the page never reuses; the page never replaces a receipt, although the store itself cannot stop another owner-level writer from replacing one, which the thread detects below. The receipt holds the proposal, the version, the build hash, the spec revision, every choice and hold with its note, the verdict and its note, the time, and the ids of the receipts it was recorded after. After recording, the page asks Daniel to send "recorded <id>" in the thread; that message wakes the thread. The thread also schedules its own checks of the store, one day after the ask and on the morning of the decide-by date, in case he records without sending it. The thread reads every receipt for the current build, keeps only those whose proposal, version, build and spec match and whose contents are valid (the id inside equal to the id it is stored under, every decision set to one of the packet's own options or hold, each note text of at most 2,000 characters and the verdict note at most 4,000, a verdict from the five, the verdict consistent with the choices as the page requires, and every predecessor a valid receipt of the same build with no loop among them), and reports any other as invalid without reading it back. The newest receipt is the valid one that no other valid receipt names as recorded after it; clocks are never used for order. When more than one is newest, the receipts compete: the thread names them, with links, and asks which stands (agent rules section 9), and the page shows them as competing and loads none. The canonical form of a receipt is the whole stored document as read, as JSON with keys sorted at every level, no spaces, UTF-8; its digest is the full sha-256 of that form, and its fingerprint, for display, is the first twelve characters of the digest. The thread posts the newest receipt quoted in full under a read-back number that is never reused in the thread (read-back 1, 2 and so on), with the fingerprint, saves the quoted copy and its full digest in the project files, and puts one decision card under it asking him to confirm read-back N as the verdict it carries: approval, approval in part, a request for changes, or not yet. His tap on that card, or his reply naming the read-back ("confirm read-back 2"), confirms exactly the quoted copy, never whatever the store holds later. The two are the same act and follow the same rule: when the thread processes either, it reads the store, and the confirmation is void if the card was withdrawn or a newer valid receipt (one recorded after the quoted one, or competing with it) is in the store at that moment; the thread then reads that receipt back. Nothing in the store shows whether a receipt was written just before or just after his tap, so the moment that counts is when the thread processes it. If the store cannot be read then, the thread says so and asks him to answer in words. To keep a withdrawn quote, he answers in words and says so ("approve as in read-back 1"), which is a word answer. A receipt id is read back at most once: if the stored contents under an id that was read back later differ from the saved digest, the thread does not quote it again; it says the record changed, withdraws the card, and asks him to record again or answer in words. A record never confirmed counts for nothing, and it never supersedes or voids an answer he gave in words or by tap. Answers also come without the store: a message of his in the thread, or a message of his in another thread of the project attached here by id (section 11), stands on its own and needs no receipt. Before acting on any answer, by card, by reply or by attached message, the thread checks that it names the current version and build of this ask (an attached message written before the current version was asked names it only if it says so), and that it is not superseded by a newer version or by a later answer of his in words or by tap. That check never needs the store, so an answer in words stands while the store is down; a confirmation of a read-back also needs the store check described above. A superseded answer approves nothing, and the thread posts a fresh read-back or asks again. When a new receipt arrives, a new version is published, or he answers in words, the thread withdraws its earlier open cards and says they are void. A receipt that arrives after he has answered is read back with a card asking whether it replaces that answer; confirming it is a change to his answer, handled as one. When two of his answers conflict, the thread does not pick one by time: following agent rules section 9, it names both, with links, and asks him which stands before acting; an answer that changes one he already confirmed is handled as a change and read back like one. The page loads a receipt into its controls only when its proposal, version, build and spec revision all match the page, and shows any other record as such without loading it; it enables recording only after the store has answered from the server, and shows a receipt only once the store has confirmed it, never this page's own unconfirmed write; it keeps every control locked while a receipt is being written, for at most 15 seconds, after which it unlocks, keeps the choices and says the outcome is unknown; the form is read, the controls locked and the write numbered before anything else happens, so no edit can slip in; a write that answers after that never replaces choices changed since, never unlocks controls a later write holds, and says which receipt was recorded with which choices; after any write it shows the receipts as the store holds them, competing ones included; when a newer receipt appears from another tab or device after Daniel started editing, it says so and needs a second tap to record after it; it applies the same validity rule as the thread to what it records and to what it reads, refuses a contradictory submission (a changed option with "approve as recommended"), derives every label from the packet itself rather than from the stored record, and picks the matching verdict as choices change; each decision whose meaning allows it offers "hold" so a partial answer is explicit; a page that cannot reach the store says so and disables recording. The thread saves the confirmed receipt, or the message, with the record bundle. Another thread's statement that he approved is not approval. Comments on the artifact, page feedback comments and a page feedback verdict on the mocks are feedback on the packet, not approval; the ask says so in one line. Silence, praise for one part, a request for suggestions and picking a design letter approve nothing. A partial answer ("approve except 3", or approving only some decisions) approves what it names: work that depends on an unapproved item waits, the status reads "Approved in part" with the approved decision numbers, and a request for changes approves nothing it does not name. Open items tagged "before launch" may stay open at approval and are asked about before release like a change. When he asks for changes, the thread revises, has the changed parts reviewed again, and asks again about the change only, carrying approved items forward without asking again. When he rejects, the thread stops and records it. If the decide-by date passes unanswered, nothing starts: the thread reminds him once and holds. Approval does not stand in for the sentences section 3 requires or for the exact-copy confirmation in agent rules section 8; the packet lists them under Implications and the ask can put them in one reply. Added Nov 13 (decision impact): An answer that picks anything other than the recommendation approves the packet as that selection: the selection page the build makes from the approved build and the picks. The thread sends Daniel that page with the read-back, or in reply to his words, and the answer counts once he confirms it. Codex then reviews the selection page and the spec as selected. The approval stands only when that review has passed with nothing consequential changed. If review changes anything consequential, the thread asks again with a new version and names each change. No implementation of the feature starts before the review has passed or the new answer is confirmed. The additions of Nov 13 apply to packets whose first ask is posted after the effective day, which the coordinator announces in the project chat once the template carries the feature, its tests pass on a sample packet, and Daniel has saved the updated feature-proposal skill. Their full rules are the spec of the approved packet (Drive: Iriai Eng, Proposals, "Decision impact in packets — proposal", https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1aD7-qR8n2GiZMRnRQHzWrldoZzZGxW2L) and the template README.
After approval. The thread sets the status to Approved (or Approved in part) with the date and the link to his words, files the approval record and the approved build in the record bundle, notes the packet link and status on the workbook row, and pins the spec revision the approval covers. The approved version is frozen in Drive by its hash; the artifact keeps one stable link and shows the current version with a line naming the approved version by its build hash and saying whether the current version is itself approved, approved in part, or pending. Completing the spec after approval may add implementation detail within the approved scope; any new consequential choice stays Proposed and goes back through review and the ask before the implementation it affects starts. Later changes are appended to part 12 as dated amendments; one that changes scope, experience, cost, data, security, compatibility, dependencies or what is hard to undo is reviewed and asked about like the original, covering the change only. Every PR for the feature links the approved packet version and its approval in its description. The PR review thread checks, before any merge or merge ask, that a feature PR links an approved packet and conforms to it; a missing approval or a departure is at least medium risk under section 7, the PR goes back to the authoring thread through the coordinator, the affected implementation pauses, and no merge word is asked for it. When Daniel's merge go-ahead in an iteration thread stands the PR review thread down on a PR (section 7), that iteration thread makes the same check before it merges; a merge word never stands in for proposal approval. Proposal approval and merge approval stay separate. After the first three feature packets are decided, the coordinator reviews the format with Daniel, with the count of feature PRs merged with and without a linked approval, the time from ask to confirmation per packet, and the thread-hours each packet took; changes to this section go through the ask like any amendment.
Skills and Docs. The feature-proposal skill (to be created with this rule) holds the packet template, the build and render script, the pre-send checks, the Codex brief and the ask format; the design-directions skill's renders feed part 3; the page-feedback skill's review links label packet mocks "<feature>-proposal-v<N>" and its verdicts stay feedback; the "Technical spec writing playbook" gains a step saying that the plain-language packet is prepared beside the spec under this section, that both are reviewed together, and that Daniel approves the packet before implementation.
17. Secrets (Daniel asked Nov 8; helper merged Nov 9)
